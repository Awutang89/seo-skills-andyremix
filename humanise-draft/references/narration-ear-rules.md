# Narration ear rules

Applies to narration and on-screen text that a listener hears or glances at once and cannot re-read.

## Narration rules

- Every sentence names its subject. No vague qualifier or leftover source clause ("at very high speed": what is?). Faithful to the source is not the same as clear to a listener.
- Explain a term right after it appears, in plain words: "[product], [what it does in plain words]", "[term], [plain gloss]".
- Spoken joins carry one fact into the next. A list of facts is a defect. "And", "But", "So" are the joins to use.
- Open on what the viewer can see, then ask why. Never state the answer before the question.

## Listening checks

The four narration rules above are this surface's wording of four general comprehension checks. Where the two wordings differ, the narration wording governs narration.

| Check | The question to ask of the text |
|---|---|
| Subject early, verb close | Does the subject come early, with its verb close behind, and does no long phrase come before the subject? |
| One new term at a time, explained where it appears | Does any sentence introduce more than one term the reader has not yet met? Is each term explained, or visibly a name to copy, where it first appears? |
| Say the relation | Where two sentences are related (contrast, cause, condition, sequence), does a word say which ("but", "so", "if", "then")? |
| Known before new | From the second sentence on, does each sentence open on something the reader already has (the last sentence's subject, a term already explained, the heading above it), with the new thing later? |

## Limits

These rules record what the company requires of its narration. No piece written under them had been published and read when they were recorded, and none is tested against viewer retention or comprehension. A finding under a listening check is a candidate for the human reader to judge, never a requirement, and a reviewer may not certify a text as clear.
