# The Intro Formula

Eight rules for the first 150–250 words. The intro is the only part of the article every reader
sees, and the only part whose job is singular: **earn the next scroll.** Nothing else in the piece
has to survive a two-second decision.

This reference expands the **First 200 Words Rule** in `SKILL.md` Phase 4 — that rule covers keyword
placement, answering the query, and stating the payoff. These eight cover the rest, and they apply
to every one of the ten content structures in Phase 3.

> A ninth rule from the source framework — *make sure these rules apply to the articles you
> internally link to* — lives in `skills/internal-linking/SKILL.md` (Phase 1, Step 1.5), because it's
> a link-target decision, not a drafting decision.

---

## 1. Match the funnel stage

**The mistake:** writing the same intro regardless of where the reader sits in the buying decision.
A comparison-stage reader doesn't need the category defined; an awareness-stage reader can't act on
a spec.

Map the brief's **Search Intent** field (`SKILL.md` Phase 2) to the intro's opening move:

| Search intent | The intro leads with | It must NOT |
|---|---|---|
| **Informational** | The answer, with the verified figure attached | Define the category from scratch or build up to the answer |
| **Commercial** | The decision the reader is actually making, plus the criteria that decide it | Stay neutral — name what separates the options |
| **Transactional** | The spec or threshold that qualifies or disqualifies them | Re-explain the product category they've already chosen |

The intent is already in the brief. This just makes it binding on the first sentence.

---

## 2. The hook — pick a type, don't drift into one

**The mistake:** an opening that's merely *accurate*. Accurate isn't interesting. The first sentence
has one job and it isn't summarising.

Three hooks that work in an institutional Direct-Expert voice:

- **Educator** — open on a verified figure from the Phase 1.5 stats list. *"[Option A] delivers
  11–14 [key spec] under load — roughly double what a same-size [option B] puts out."*
- **Consequence** — open on the cost of getting it wrong. This is the source framework's "fear"
  hook, reframed: consequence-first belongs in a Direct-Expert register, so no manufactured alarm is
  needed. Real numbers do the work. *"Undersizing by 20% doesn't mean slower work. It means the
  [component] cycles until it fails, usually inside two years."*
- **Contrarian** — state the take the SERP won't. Requires the heavier corroboration described under
  *Win the Arbitration* in `SKILL.md` Phase 4 — a contrarian opening is a contrarian claim.

**Humor** is the fourth type in the source framework. It's listed here for completeness and marked
**off-register** for an institutional voice assessing a considered purchase — comic openers fight
the register. Skip it unless the brand voice profile says otherwise.

Whichever you pick, the hook is the **first sentence**, not the third.

---

## 3. Tease the payoff — without the table of contents

**The mistake:** either no payoff at all, or a payoff phrased as an agenda.

The payoff itself is already required (First 200 Words Rule, item 3). What this adds is the
*phrasing* rule, because the obvious way to write it collides with rules already in the skill:

**Banned phrasings** — these are Summary Sandwich tells and sit on the Phase 5 banned-phrase list:

- "By the end of this article, you're going to learn how to…"
- "This comprehensive guide will…"
- "In this post we'll cover…"
- "Here's what we'll look at…"

**Write the payoff as an outcome, not an agenda.** State what the reader will be able to *decide* or
*do*, in the same breath as the hook where possible:

> Bad: "By the end of this guide, you'll learn about [key spec], [attribute], and [attribute]."
>
> Good: "Get the [key spec] number right and the rest of the spec sheet stops mattering — you'll be
> able to size the [product] off two figures you already know."

One sentence. It is a promise, not a contents page.

---

## 4. Establish authority — one clause, institutional

**The mistake:** writing with expertise but never showing where it comes from, so the reader has no
reason to prefer this page over the nine others.

One clause in the intro showing **how we know** — the equivalent of the E-E-A-T *HOW* gate, applied
at the top of the page instead of buried in section four:

- Sourcing: *"…per the manufacturer's published ratings"*
- Scope: *"…across the 40+ [product]s we carry"*
- Method: *"…measured at [standard test condition], the only rating that's comparable across brands"*

**Constraint — no invented bylines.** The E-E-A-T pre-gate in `SKILL.md` Phase 7 bans author
personas outright. Authority here is institutional ("we carry," "we've specified," "in the field"),
never a fabricated named expert. If there is no genuine authority signal, say what the claim rests
on instead — a named primary source is a legitimate authority signal.

---

## 5. State the method — comparison, listicle, roundup, review

**The mistake:** publishing a ranking without saying what produced the ranking, which is exactly what
makes affiliate roundups untrustworthy — and what makes AI answer engines discard them (see
*The Primary-Source Register* in `SKILL.md` Phase 5).

**Required for:** Comparison, Listicle, Product-Review, Data-Research, and anything shaped like a
"best X" roundup. **Optional elsewhere.**

Put the selection or ranking criteria in the intro, in one line, with the threshold visible:

> "Ranked on [key spec] at [standard test condition], [attribute], and cost per [unit] over five
> years — not on brand, and not on what's in stock."

The Listicle structure already asks for "how items were selected" and Data-Research already asks for
a Methodology section. This generalizes both, and moves the statement into the intro where it
actually buys trust.

Do not parrot the criteria other reviews use. If the criteria are the same as everyone else's, the
ranking will be too.

---

## 6. Get straight to the point

Already enforced — First 200 Words Rule ("answer the query in the first 2–3 sentences") and the
*Opening leads with the point* check in the Phase 7 Editorial Line-Edit Audit. No new rule.

Restated only because it's the rule the other seven most often break: a hook that takes three
sentences to arrive is not a hook.

---

## 7. Length — cap it by structure

**The mistake:** an intro that keeps going because it's the easiest part to write.

Write however long it takes to hook the reader, then stop. Caps from the Phase 3 structures:

| Structure | Intro budget |
|---|---|
| Pillar Guide | 150–250 words |
| How-To Tutorial | 150–200 words (What You'll Achieve) |
| Comparison | 200–300 words (Quick Verdict) |
| Listicle | 150–200 words |
| Data-Research | 150–200 words (Headline Finding) |
| News-Analysis | 100–150 words (What Happened) |
| Product-Review | 150–250 words (Verdict First) |
| Case-Study | 150–250 words (The Situation) |
| Thought-Leadership | 150–250 words (The Take) |

The TL;DR box sits *after* the intro and doesn't count against these.

---

## 8. No links before the first H2

**The mistake:** offering the reader a door out of the page before they've committed to the page.

**The rule:** no links in the intro — internal or external. The intro's only goal is the next scroll,
and every link is an invitation to leave.

Notes:

- `internal-linking` already enforces this for internal links: Phase 3, Step 3.1 excludes the intro,
  FAQ, and conclusion from link placement. This extends the same rule to **external** links, which
  nothing previously covered.
- The source framework carves out an exception for monetized links. Not adopted: collection and
  product links are subject to the same rule, and the publish step applies nofollow to external links
  regardless. Money links belong in the body and the CTA, where the reader has already been earned.
- Enforcement is a **soft flag**, not a QA gate FAIL — an intro link is caught in the Editorial
  Line-Edit Audit table (`SKILL.md` Phase 7) and cleared by the reviewer, not blocked at file
  generation.

## Build order

Five moves, in this order:

1. **Hook** — educator, consequence, or contrarian. First sentence.
2. **Answer** — the query, directly, with the primary keyword used once and naturally.
3. **Payoff** — what they'll be able to decide or do. An outcome, not an agenda.
4. **Authority** — one clause on how we know.
5. **Method** — the selection or ranking criteria (comparison / listicle / roundup / review only).

Then stop, and let the TL;DR box do the summarising.

---

## Worked example

**Before** — fails hooks 1, 2, 4, 5; breaks rules 3 and 8:

> In today's industrial landscape, choosing the right [product] is a crucial decision for any
> business. There are many factors to consider, including size, power, and type. In this
> comprehensive guide, we'll explore everything you need to know about
> [[product] sizing](https://example.com/blog/how-to-choose-product) so you can make an informed
> decision.

**After** — consequence hook, answer, outcome payoff, authority clause, criteria, zero links:

> Undersizing a [product] by 20% doesn't make the work slower. It makes the [component] cycle until
> it fails, usually inside two years. Sizing a [product] comes down to one number — the [key spec]
> your [use case] actually draws — and once you have it, the rest of the spec sheet falls out of it.
> The figures below come from manufacturer published ratings across the 40+ units we carry, compared
> at [standard test condition] because it's the only rating that's consistent between brands.

91 words. Hook in the first sentence, keyword once, payoff as an outcome, authority in a clause,
comparison basis stated, no links.
